How to play

The short version

You have a plot of lunar surface at the rim of a lava-tube skylight, and a colony that needs feeding. Pick a tool on the left, then click — or drag — on the ground. Raise grow halls, sow them, keep them watered and fed, harvest before anything wilts, and spend the proceeds on the hardware that gets you through the night.

Making a field

Take the Grow Hall tool and drag a rectangle across open ground — the outline shows the size and the price as you go, green if it will fit and red if something is in the way. Let go and the hall goes up. A hall is one field: it takes a single crop across its whole floor, and cost, yield, lighting draw and water all scale with how many tiles you gave it. Halls can be anything from a single tile up to eight a side, so you can put a small research hall next to a sprawling potato barn.

Tracks matter

A hall or module has to touch a regolith track that leads back to a habitat, or the crew cannot service it properly and it grows at about three-quarters speed. Lay track as you expand — it is the cheapest thing you can build. Unserviced modules show a red pip.

The lunar night is the whole game

A lunar day runs about 29.5 Earth days: roughly fourteen and a half sunlit, then the same again in darkness. Solar arrays give you nothing for two weeks at a stretch. A legacy radioisotope unit covers baseline life support but not the grow lamps, so power is shed one whole hall at a time as the batteries drain. Battery banks buy you a few halls through the night; fission surface power ends the problem for good.

The loop is closed

Plants make oxygen and consume carbon dioxide; the crew do the reverse. A surplus farm exports carbon inside the food it sells, so the buffer runs down over time — watch the CO₂ figure, build the biomass oxidation loop, and buy a cylinder if it gets tight. Water is recycled at about eighty percent; the rest comes from resupply or from cooking hydrogen out of regolith.

Reading a hall

The bar along the bottom of a hall is growth. Coloured pips along the top mean, in order: green for ready to harvest, yellow for fungal infection, blue for dry, purple for starved of nutrients, red for no track connection. Select any module to see the full picture.

Things worth knowing

  • Photoperiod is a lever, not a setting. Longer days grow crops faster and cost proportionally more power.
  • Leafy greens are quick, cheap and barely feed anyone. Potato, sweetpotato, wheat and soybean are what close the calorie gap.
  • Duckweed and spirulina are fast enough to finish inside a single lunar day. Plant them before sunset.
  • Zinnias feed nobody and are worth planting anyway — crew morale is a real constraint.
  • Arabidopsis pays in science, which is what unlocks the serious hardware.
  • Fungal infection spreads to neighbouring halls if you leave it.
  • Surplus oxygen sells. So does surplus food, but selling food exports carbon.

Credits

An original game, written from scratch. It owes its shape to Maxis' SimFarm (1993), but shares no code or art with it. The crop roster is drawn from plants that have actually flown or are long-standing candidates for bioregenerative life support; the numbers are tuned for play, not for publication.
